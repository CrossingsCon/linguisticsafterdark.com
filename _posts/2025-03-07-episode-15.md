- [The New England historical accent grid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_England_English#/media/File:New_England_English_map.jpg) Sarah’s husband has mentioned is real! Eli’s guesses were backwards, however—the north–south distinctions are different vowel mergers, while the east–west distinctions are rhoticity: Northern New England accents have typically had the [cot–caught merger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cot%E2%80%93caught_merger), while southern New England accents have tended to have the [father–bother](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonological_history_of_English_open_back_vowels#Father%E2%80%93bother_merger) merger; eastern New England is historically non-rhotic, while western New England is historically r-ful
- Eli was also wrong about the [pin–pen merger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonological_history_of_English_close_front_vowels#Pin%E2%80%93pen_merger); it’s one of the most well-known features of Southern American English, not at all associated with New England. (He may have been thinking of the cot–caught merger, which *is* most common in the northeastern US?)

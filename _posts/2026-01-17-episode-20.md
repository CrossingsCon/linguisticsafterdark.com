## Links and other post-show thoughts:
- [WaniKani](https://www.wanikani.com/)
- Yes, being in Italy during Easter during the Jubilee was wild; being in the Vatican while the Pope laid in state was even wilder. (No, Sarah and her students did not get to see him, because they did not have 3+ hours to stand in line)
- “Ghosts speak Latin” was supposedly a common belief in Elizabethan England, although Jenny couldn’t actually find a source more direct/reliable than footnotes in various copies of Hamlet (I.i.49) so better citation possibly needed?
- [Ubbi dubbi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ubbi_dubbi), [Polari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polari), [Tutnese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutnese), and [Verlan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verlan) We couldn’t find an actual concrete origin of the name [“Pig Latin,”](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pig_Latin#Origins_and_history) though it does seem to have been called “Hog Latin” and to have begun as a version of “Dog Latin” (which seems to have referred to parody Latin in general).
- The Pig Latin puzzler was [episode 8](https://linguisticsafterdark.com/2024/03/22/episode-8.html)!
- Sarah says that the N in ‘a whole nother’ is always there. This is false: around twenty minutes earlier, Sarah herself said “a whole other,” which in retrospect she can confirm is absolutely influenced by Al’s point that knowing how the spelling works can change your pronunciation.
- [Some examples of Verlan](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/XbdWYNpQC2c) from one of the French YouTube channels Sarah mentioned; the other channel she was thinking of was [royaventurera](https://www.youtube.com/@royaventurera).
- “Helicopter” came up in [episode 14](https://linguisticsafterdark.com/2024/12/28/episode-14.html)
- “[Electric](https://www.etymonline.com/word/electric)” does derive from Greek “ēlektron,” but not directly! It was borrowed into Latin as “electrum,” and then “electricus” in the 1600s by physicist William Gilbert. “Electric” and “[electricity](https://www.etymonline.com/word/electricity)” were both first used in English in the 1640s by Sir Thomas Browne, who seems to have coined the latter. “[Electrick](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/electrick#English)” is a known early alternate spelling, so that definitely always would have been pronounced with a [k], but we couldn’t find evidence in either direction for whether “electricity” has always had an [s]. Also, Jenny has had [the Schoolhouse Rock song](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xu_cdePrNe8) stuck in her head for the last three days.
- The conlang Eli was thinking of is the Aramteskan language from P.M. Freestone’s [Shadowscent](https://www.goodreads.com/series/243061-shadowscent) series, and [was developed by Lauren Gawne](https://www.superlinguo.com/post/188957453957/how-i-made-the-aramteskan-language-for-pm), of [Lingthusiasm](https://www.superlinguo.com/post/188477210640/lingthusiasm-lingthusiasm-episode-37-smell) fame!
- [Dual, trial, paucal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_number#Dual)
- [The NativLang](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eelVqfm8vVc&t=4m36s) video Sarah referenced that discusses Chinese timekeeping words: the old system measured the day in twelve equal 時 (shí), or in 100 equal 刻 (kè) ([which could be subdivided into 分 (fēn)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_timekeeping#F%C4%93n)), although how long a fēn was varied over time). Today, 時 is used for hours (24 / day), 刻 for quarter-hours, and 分 for minutes (60 / hour).
